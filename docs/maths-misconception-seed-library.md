## 1. Place Value & Decimals

**1. Zero placeholder missing** — Writing "three hundred and four" as 34.
*Hint:* Say the number out loud. Have you left an empty column where a zero needs to stand guard?

**2. Zero placeholder added incorrectly** — Writing "three hundred and four" as 3004.
*Hint:* Check your columns. Hundreds, tens, and ones should only take up three spaces, not four.

**3. Decimal alignment** — Aligning numbers by their right-most digit rather than the decimal point (e.g., adding 3.4 to 12.56 by lining up the 4 and the 6).
*Hint:* Make sure the decimal points are lined up in a perfectly straight line before you start adding.

**4. Longer decimals are larger** — Believing 0.345 is larger than 0.4 because it has more digits.
*Hint:* Look at the tenths column first. Which number has more tenths?

**5. Shorter decimals are larger** — Believing 0.4 is larger than 0.45 because tenths are bigger than hundredths.
*Hint:* Try filling in the empty spaces with a zero. Which is bigger, 0.40 or 0.45?

**6. Multiplying by 10 adds a zero** — Applying this rule to decimals (e.g., 3.4 × 10 = 3.40).
*Hint:* When we multiply by 10, the digits move one place to the left. A zero only goes on the end if there are empty spaces before the decimal point!

**7. Decimals as two separate whole numbers** — Thinking 3.12 is larger than 3.8 because 12 is bigger than 8.
*Hint:* The decimal point separates wholes from parts. Compare the parts column by column, starting with the tenths.

**8. Negative number inversion** — Believing −10 is greater than −5.
*Hint:* Think of a thermometer. Which temperature is colder and further down the scale?

**9. Rounding misdirection** — Always rounding down regardless of the digit.
*Hint:* Look at the next digit door. If it is 5 or more, you need to round up!

**10. Rounding exact halves** — Rounding numbers ending in .5 down instead of up.
*Hint:* Remember the rule: if it sits exactly halfway, we always round up to the next number.

## 2. Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication & Division

**11. Commutative subtraction** — Always subtracting the smaller digit from the larger one in column subtraction, regardless of position (e.g., for 42 − 17, doing 7 − 2 = 5).
*Hint:* Read the sum from top to bottom. If the top number is smaller, you need to exchange!

**12. Forgetting to exchange** — Leaving the column as a zero or skipping it when the top number is smaller.
*Hint:* If you do not have enough in the top column, you must borrow from the next column over.

**13. Adding the exchange twice** — Counting the carried '1' in addition as a full 10 or adding it twice.
*Hint:* Cross out your carried number once you have added it so you do not count it again.

**14. The '=' sign as "the answer is"** — Failing on missing number problems (e.g., 4 + 5 = _ + 2).
*Hint:* The equals sign means "is exactly the same as". Both sides of the sign must balance like a set of scales.

**15. Multiplication always makes it bigger** — Believing this is a universal rule (becomes an issue with fractions/decimals).
*Hint:* Multiplying means finding "groups of" a number. What happens if you find half a group of something?

**16. Reversing division** — Thinking 3 ÷ 12 is the same as 12 ÷ 3 because division is commutative.
*Hint:* Order matters in division! Are you sharing 12 sweets between 3 people, or 3 sweets between 12 people?

**17. Decimal remainders** — Writing a remainder as a decimal directly (e.g., 13 ÷ 5 = 2 r 3, so writing 2.3).
*Hint:* A remainder is what is left over, but a decimal is a specific fraction. Try writing your remainder as a fraction instead.

**18. Missing long multiplication placeholder** — Forgetting the zero when multiplying the tens digit.
*Hint:* You are multiplying by a ten, not a one! What needs to go in the ones column to show this?

**19. Context-blind division** — Ignoring the reality of the question (e.g., 21 children, 5 per car, 21 ÷ 5 = 4 r 1, so answering 4 cars).
*Hint:* Look at what the question is asking. If there is a remainder, where will those extra people sit? You might need to round up.

**20. Factors vs Multiples** — Swapping the definitions.
*Hint:* Factors fit into the number. Multiples are the times tables that multiply up and away!

## 3. Fractions, Decimals & Percentages

**21. Larger denominator means larger fraction** — Believing 1/8 > 1/4.
*Hint:* The bottom number tells you how many slices the cake is cut into. If you cut it into more slices, what happens to the size of each slice?

**22. Adding numerators and denominators** — e.g., 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5.
*Hint:* You can only add fractions when the bottom numbers are exactly the same. Find a common denominator first!

**23. Additive equivalent fractions** — Adding 2 to the top and bottom instead of multiplying (e.g., 1/2 = 3/4).
*Hint:* Whatever you multiply the top by, you must multiply the bottom by.

**24. Inverted fraction of an amount** — Multiplying by the denominator and dividing by the numerator.
*Hint:* Divide by the bottom to find one part, then multiply by the top to find out how many parts you need.

**25. Confusing thirds and tenths** — Thinking 1/3 is equal to 0.3.
*Hint:* 0.3 means three-tenths (3/10). Does one-third equal three-tenths?

**26. Percentage symbol as a unit** — Just sticking a % sign on a decimal without converting (e.g., 0.4 = 0.4%).
*Hint:* Percent means "out of 100". To turn a decimal into a percentage, you need to multiply it by 100.

**27. Improper fractions are "wrong"** — Trying to convert an improper fraction to a mixed number before multiplying, causing calculation errors.
*Hint:* Top-heavy fractions are perfectly fine for working out the maths. You only need to turn them back at the very end.

**28. Fractions as two separate numbers** — Treating 3/4 as just a '3' and a '4' sitting near each other.
*Hint:* A fraction is a single number. The line in the middle just means "divide".

**29. Comparing without common denominators** — Looking only at the numerators (e.g., 3/8 > 2/3 because 3 is bigger than 2).
*Hint:* You cannot compare them fairly until they are speaking the same language. Make the bottom numbers match first.

**30. Fractions of different wholes** — Believing 1/4 of a small shape is the exact same size as 1/4 of a large shape.
*Hint:* Fractions are about proportions, not exact sizes. A quarter of a small pizza is not the same amount of food as a quarter of a massive pizza!

## 4. Measurement (Length, Mass, Volume, Time, Money)

**31. Dropping the zero in money** — Writing £3.5 instead of £3.50.
*Hint:* Money always needs two decimal places to show the pennies. Add a placeholder zero!

**32. Base-100 time** — Calculating time as if there are 100 minutes in an hour (e.g., 2:45 + 20 mins = 2:65).
*Hint:* There are only 60 minutes in an hour. Once you hit 60, you need to roll over into the next hour.

**33. 12/24-hour confusion** — Writing 12:00 for midnight or 00:00 for midday.
*Hint:* In the 24-hour clock, the day starts at exactly 00:00 (midnight).

**34. Starting measurement at 1** — Placing the edge of the object at the '1' mark on a ruler instead of the '0'.
*Hint:* Always line up the edge of your object with the zero line, not the end of the ruler or the number one.

**35. Assuming scale ticks are 1** — Not calculating the scale intervals on a graph or jug.
*Hint:* Look at the numbers provided. How much space is between them, and how many jumps are there? Divide the space by the jumps!

**36. Multiplying to convert to a larger unit** — e.g., 100cm = 10,000m.
*Hint:* A metre is much larger than a centimetre, so you will need fewer of them. If you are going to a larger unit, you need to divide.

**37. Metric prefix confusion** — Thinking 1kg = 100g.
*Hint:* "Kilo" always means one thousand. There are 1,000 grams in a kilogram.

**38. Perimeter vs Area (Adding)** — Adding the sides together when asked for area.
*Hint:* Perimeter is the fence around the outside (add the sides). Area is the grass inside (multiply the length by the width).

**39. Perimeter vs Area (Multiplying)** — Multiplying all visible numbers together when asked for perimeter.
*Hint:* To find the perimeter, imagine you are an ant walking all the way around the very edge. Add up the journey!

**40. Area dictates perimeter** — Believing that if two shapes have an area of 12cm², they must both have the same perimeter.
*Hint:* Try drawing two different shapes with an area of 12 squares. Now count the outside edges of both — are they the same?

## 5. Geometry (Properties of Shape, Position, Direction)

**41. Orientation changes shape** — Thinking a rotated square is a "diamond" and no longer a square.
*Hint:* Turn your paper around. A shape keeps its true name no matter which way it is pointing.

**42. Square/Rectangle exclusivity** — Believing a square is absolutely not a type of rectangle.
*Hint:* A rectangle has four right angles and opposite equal sides. Does a square have those? Yes! A square is just a very special rectangle.

**43. Line length dictates angle** — Believing the physical length of the drawn lines changes the size of the angle.
*Hint:* An angle measures the amount of turn between two lines, not how long the lines are drawn.

**44. Reading the wrong protractor scale** — Confusing the inner and outer numbers, resulting in an acute angle being labelled 130°.
*Hint:* Look at the angle first. Is it smaller or larger than a perfect right angle (90°)? Use that to choose the right number!

**45. Diagonals as symmetry** — Thinking a standard rectangle has 4 lines of symmetry because of the diagonals.
*Hint:* If you folded a rectangle along its diagonal line, would the corners match up perfectly?

**46. Reversed coordinates** — Reading the y-axis before the x-axis.
*Hint:* Remember the golden rule: you must go along the corridor (x) before you go up the stairs (y).

**47. Translation by gap** — Counting the squares between two shapes instead of point-to-point.
*Hint:* Pick one single corner on the first shape, and count the squares to the exact same corner on the new shape.

**48. Reflection as sliding** — Just sliding the shape across the mirror line instead of flipping it.
*Hint:* A mirror flips everything backwards. The point closest to the mirror line on the first shape must be the closest point on the reflected shape.

**49. Universal symmetry** — Assuming all triangles have a vertical line of symmetry.
*Hint:* Only isosceles and equilateral triangles have lines of symmetry. A scalene triangle has none at all!

**50. Faces vs Edges** — Confusing the flat surfaces with the straight lines where they meet.
*Hint:* A face is the flat part you can put a sticker on. An edge is the sharp part you could run your finger along.

## 6. Statistics, Ratio & Proportion

**51. Additive scaling** — Adding instead of multiplying to scale up a ratio/recipe (e.g., if 2 eggs make 4 cakes, 4 eggs make 6 cakes by adding 2).
*Hint:* Recipes scale up by multiplication. If you double the eggs, you must double the cakes too!

**52. Ratio to fraction confusion** — If the ratio of red to blue is 1:3, thinking the fraction of red is 1/3.
*Hint:* A fraction is "part out of the whole". Add the ratio parts together first to find the whole total (1 + 3 = 4, so it is 1/4).

**53. Ignoring the pictogram key** — Assuming 1 picture = 1 item, regardless of the key.
*Hint:* Always check the key before you start counting! Sometimes one picture stands for 2, 5, or even 10 things.

**54. Reading the gap** — Looking at the white space instead of the top of the bar in a bar chart.
*Hint:* Follow the flat top line of the bar straight across to the axis to read the true number.

**55. Ignoring line graph scale divisions** — Reading the axis numbers but ignoring what the smaller tick marks represent.
*Hint:* Do not just guess where the line sits. Work out exactly what one little square is worth on the axis first.

**56. Incomplete mean** — Adding all the numbers together but forgetting to divide.
*Hint:* The mean is about sharing things out equally. Once you have your total, you must divide by how many items there are.

**57. Mean vs Median** — Just picking the middle number in the list.
*Hint:* The middle number is the median. To find the mean, you need to add them all up and divide!

**58. Restrictive mean** — Thinking the calculated mean must be one of the original numbers in the list.
*Hint:* The mean is an average. It is perfectly fine if the final answer is a decimal or a number that wasn't in your original list.

**59. Fixed pie chart wedges** — Thinking the physical size of a pie chart wedge represents a fixed number rather than a proportion (e.g., "this wedge is the same size as the one on the other chart, so it means 10 people too").
*Hint:* A pie chart only shows fractions of a total. You need to know the grand total before you can figure out what the slices mean!

**60. Ignoring the outside of a Venn diagram** — Forgetting to record items that fit into neither category.
*Hint:* Every piece of data needs a home! If it doesn't fit into any of the circles, it must be written in the box on the outside.
