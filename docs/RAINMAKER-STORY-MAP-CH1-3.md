## PART B — CHAPTERS 1–3 (schema-shaped; seeded Vault words in **bold**; audio pending; spot briefs attached)

### CHAPTER 1 — "The Borrowed Coat"
`{id: s1-ch1, trigger: rank:TRAINEE, seededWords: [peculiar, reluctant, threshold, precise], spot: spot-borrowed-coat (BUILT), choice: ch1-desk}`

The coat is too big. You noticed that before you noticed anything else — before the rain, before the brass sign, before the door with CLUECREW painted on the glass in gold.

"It belonged to somebody good," says the woman behind the desk, without looking up. "Grow into it."

This is Chief Inspector Marlowe. Her office smells of tea and pencil shavings. On the wall behind her there are photographs of detectives shaking hands with mayors, and one empty hook where a photograph used to be.

You stand at the **threshold** and drip.

"Well," she says. "Come in or go out. Doors are for one or the other."

You come in.

On her desk there is a letter. The paper is thick and cream-coloured, and the writing is small and very **precise**, like stitches. Marlowe turns it around so you can read it.

*The rain over Lantern Street will stop at fourteen minutes past four.*

"That's it?" you say. "That's the whole letter?"

"That's the fourth one this month." Marlowe looks at the clock. It says 4:12. "They're always right. That's the **peculiar** part. Predicting rain is easy. Predicting the *minute* — " she taps the letter " — that's something else."

A man bursts in sideways, carrying nine folders and wearing his glasses on top of his head. "Chief, I can't find my glasses, and also the postmarks are wrong, and also — oh." He sees you. He beams like a lamp. "The new recruit! Excellent. Terrible weather. Wonderful coat."

"Ozzie Finch," says Marlowe. "Codes. Occasionally chaos."

Outside, the rain stops.

You look at the clock: 4:14. Exactly. Not one minute early, not one late.

Ozzie whistles softly. Marlowe watches you instead of the window — watching what you do with it. You could say something clever, or something honest, or nothing at all. You get the feeling, with Marlowe, that honest wins.

> **CHOICE (cosmetic):** "Who's writing them?" / "How many letters are there?" / Say nothing and look at the postmark.
> *(Each earns one dry Marlowe line; all end the same:)*

"Good," says Marlowe, in the tone of somebody ticking a box. "You notice things. Let's see what you notice." She slides a case file across the desk. It has your name on it. It is the first thing in Ashline, apart from the coat, that is exactly your size.

Somewhere across the city, somebody is already writing the next letter — and is **reluctant**, for now, to be found.

### CHAPTER 2 — "The Postmark Problem"
`{id: s1-ch2, trigger: cases_cracked:3, seededWords: [decipher, baffled, methodical, jumble], spot: brief below, choice: ch2-desk}`

Ozzie's desk looks like a paper storm surrendered there. But watch him for a minute and you see the secret: it is a **methodical** storm. Codes on the left. Letters in the middle. A mug of cold tea holding down anything important.

"Postmarks," he says, the moment you sit down. "Every letter is stamped with the street it was posted from. Standard. Boring. Except." He fans five envelopes out like playing cards. "Tilbrook Row. Cavern Lane. Old Spindle Street."

You wait. He waits back, eyebrows up, delighted.

"I give up," you say.

"They don't exist!" He says it like a magic trick. "No Tilbrook Row anywhere in Ashline. Never has been. I've checked the maps back ninety years — the maps are under the tea, don't move the tea. Five letters, five streets, and not one of them is real."

"So the postmarks are fake."

"Or," says Ozzie, holding up one finger, "they're a **jumble**. Letters moved. Letters swapped. A real street, wearing a disguise." He slides a fresh sheet of paper across to you, and on it he has written TILBROOK ROW in large hopeful capitals. "I've been staring at it since Tuesday. I am, officially, **baffled**. And my glasses are missing again, which never helps."

They are on his head. You decide to let him find that out himself.

Here is the thing you are learning about the Rainmaker: whoever they are, they don't make mistakes. If the streets are in disguise, the disguise can be taken off — by somebody who knows how codes come apart. Somebody with sharp eyes and a desk of their own.

> **CHOICE (cosmetic):** "Give it here." / "Have you tried working backwards?" / Quietly move his glasses down onto his nose.

Ozzie grins and pushes the whole stack towards you. "That's the spirit. Off you go — this is a job for the code desk." He taps your case file. "Your desk. **Decipher** the streets, and we'll know where the Rainmaker really walks."

### CHAPTER 3 — "The Shop-Sign Stowaways"
`{id: s1-ch3, trigger: rank:JUNIOR_DETECTIVE, seededWords: [conceal, prominent, vigilant, courier], spot: brief below, choice: ch3-bakery, plants: GREY_UMBRELLA_TOKEN_1}`

The Cipher Quarter is different at dusk. The shop signs light up one by one — the bakery, the mapmaker's, the umbrella shop that never seems to sell any umbrellas — and the wet cobbles hold every colour like spilled paint.

You are here because of the sixth letter. It said: *The answer hides where everyone looks and no one sees.*

"Which is either very deep," said Ozzie, "or shop signs. I'd bet my glasses on shop signs. Whatever they're worth. I can't find them."

So now you and he walk the Quarter, reading. And once you start really reading, you can't stop seeing it: little words stowed away inside big ones, hiding in **prominent** places, letters shared between one word's end and the next word's start. Perfectly **concealed** — except from somebody trained to look.

"There." Ozzie stops so fast his folders concertina. He points at the bakery: FORTUNE & CRUMB, EST. 1911. His finger underlines it slowly. "Somebody," he says, "has been leaving us breadcrumbs. In the bakery sign. I want you to appreciate the Rainmaker has a sense of humour."

Inside, the baker is closing up. Yes, she got a letter last week. Yes, it came true — it said her Tuesday delivery would arrive on Wednesday, and it did, and honestly it always does, so it wasn't much of a prediction. She laughs. Then she stops laughing.

"Funny thing, though. The **courier** said somebody had already signed for it. Somebody who knew it was coming." She shrugs. "Left before I could thank them. Left that behind, too — been meaning to throw it out."

She points behind the flour sacks.

It is a token — small, round, grey, stamped with a tiny umbrella. It is cold in your hand, and oddly heavy, the way important things sometimes are.

Ozzie goes quiet, which for Ozzie is an event. "Now that," he says at last, "is new."

> **CHOICE (cosmetic):** Pocket the token carefully. / "Has anyone else been asking questions?" / Look back at the sign one more time.

You walk back through the lit-up Quarter, **vigilant** now in a way you weren't an hour ago, reading every sign twice. Somebody got here first. Somebody is always getting there first.

At HQ, Marlowe turns the token over once. Then she opens a drawer, takes out a pin — a small brass magnifying glass — and slides it across the desk.

"Junior Detective," she says. "The coat's looking less borrowed already."

## AUTHORING RULES EXTRACTED (bind all future chapters — v1.1, upgraded after David's sophistication challenge)
1. **Cold open law:** the impossible/curious thing lands in the first five lines; furniture and introductions follow incident, never precede it. (Ch1–3 as drafted require this rewrite before review.)
2. **End on a question, never on peril:** every chapter closes on a curiosity gap — something the child *needs to know* — with warmth intact. "Warm and forward" endings without an open question fail review. Anxiety cliffhangers remain banned (Tension Law).
3. **Reading-age ladder:** Ch1 ≈ 8.5 rising to Ch7 ≈ 10; the prose grows up as the detective ranks up. Audio (Law 4) carries access at every level.
4. **REAL-PUZZLE LAW:** every named cipher, code, jumbled street, or hidden word in prose must be a genuine, solvable, 11+-grade item — authored through the item pipeline at proper tier, reviewed like an item, with its solution paying off on-page in the corresponding case wrappers. Decorative puzzles are banned; the story never bluffs. (TILBROOK ROW, CAVERN LANE, OLD SPINDLE STREET and FORTUNE & CRUMB must be re-authored as real anagrams/hidden words before Ch2–3 publish.)
5. **One optional clue-tap per chapter:** a single in-reader interactive beat (tap the hidden word, decode one street) using live engine logic — no scoring, no mastery writes, skippable. The taste in the story; the meal in the cases (Law 1 amended accordingly).
6. Chapters set leads and end by sending the child to the cases — nothing beyond the single clue-tap is ever solved in prose.
7. One umbrella plant per ledger entry only; discovered, never assigned.
8. Seeded words: 4–6 per chapter, natural placement, never defined in-prose.
9. Ozzie is wrong confidently and delightedly; Marlowe speaks in ticked boxes; nobody tells the child they're clever — the world responds as if they are.
10. **ANTI-MARKER LAW (David's list, enforced in CI):** chapter prose runs through a style scanner before review. Hard budgets and bans: **em dashes ≤2 per chapter, none in narration** (dialogue-interruption only) · **no abstraction-tag endings** ("...the way important things sometimes are", "...as if the world itself", "...somehow") · **triads ≤1 per chapter** (list-of-three constructions flagged for human judgement) · **no echo constructions** ("Somebody X. Somebody is always X-ing") · banned phrases: "little did", "testament to", "couldn't help but", "a mix of", "palpable", "unspoken", "in that moment" · **repeated signature phrases across chapters flagged** (the scanner keeps a phrase memory; "not one minute early, not one late" may exist once in the season, not twice). Scanner output is advisory-plus-budget: budget breaches block review submission; advisory flags require the human writer's dismissal note. **Final filter remains David's read-aloud pass — the scanner catches patterns, the novelist catches soul.**
