# Authoring brief addendum — the remaining nineteen clusters

Three things that came out of the pilot and the T4 census, in the order they bite.

---

## 1. A passage's own suggestions are never the item

**THE RESTRICTION — one rule, both fields, no exceptions.**

> A passage file's suggestion fields — Stream A's `questionHooks`, Stream B's
> `craftNotes.complication` — are **evidence that the passage has something worth
> questioning. They are never the question.** No T4 item may be drafted from either
> field, on any of the nineteen remaining clusters. Read them to decide a passage is
> worth working. Find the item by reading the prose.

That is the whole instruction. The rest of this section is why it is a restriction
rather than a caution, and it is one failure mode, not two: **a field that proposes an
item has not been through the checks that decide whether it is one.** The two fields
differ only in how convincing they are while failing.

**`questionHooks` fails visibly.** Twelve suggested items per passage, typed retrieval /
inference / vocabulary-in-context / technique. Three auditors working independently on
different passages reported the same fault unprompted: the hooks are T2/T3 shaped
throughout, and several label as *inference* something the passage states outright.

- Hound of the Baskervilles: the armed-guards hook — the answer is the content of a
  later span. Long-range retrieval, not inference.
- The Jungle Book: "why the narrator says Nag was afraid" — the text states the reason.
- Mill on the Floss: "why Tom is cross" — stated almost verbatim at lines 47-49.

**None of the genuine T4 items the census found came from a hook.** Not one.

The consequence if hooks drive drafting is not hypothetical — it has already happened:
six of the seven T4 items in ENG-001 and ENG-002 were re-tiered to T3 for exactly this
fault, an item that looks hard because the wording is dense while sitting on a single
span that states its own answer. Unchecked, that reproduces across nineteen more
clusters before human review sees any of it.

**`craftNotes.complication` fails invisibly, and is the more dangerous of the two.** It
is usually RIGHT about the passage while being wrong about the item — and it is written
in almost exactly the shape the two-halved-contrast test looks for, so **it defeats
scrutiny by looking pre-validated.** A drafter who has just learned the contrast test
reads this field and sees the test already passed. It has not been run.

The complete census checked all five commissioned passages against the real test:

- **b-02 (Saltmere)** — the note promises Cal's stated reason undermined by the unsent
  photograph. That contrast is genuinely in the text, and it **fails the plot-summary
  check**: a child told only the plot reaches "he really went for himself" without
  reading a line. The real item is invisible to summary — see §3.
- **b-01 (wasps)** — the note promises a two-dangers gap between adult and child. The
  narrator **states that gap outright at lines 39-40**, which kills it — the same
  narratorial-signposting failure that killed Wind in the Willows and Eighty Days in
  Stream A. The surviving item is that the writer never once uses the word *wasp*,
  supplying the hum, the counting and the nest instead.
- **b-03, b-04, b-05** — delivered as promised; b-03's note understates it.

The commissioning brief works: all five passages DO support a whole-text item. What the
field cannot tell you is *which* item — because the four checks the two-halved test does
not replace (plot-summary, three separate instances, half-pattern distractor,
writer-not-story subject) are exactly the ones it has never been through.

**The rate: two of five — 40% of the commissioned passages.** b-01 and b-02 fail;
b-03, b-04 and b-05 are delivered as promised.

> *Settled 2026-08-11.* The census's summary sentence — "the wrong item in two of the
> five, and a non-item in one" — reads as either two or three depending on whether the
> non-item is one OF the two or a third case. Its itemised bullets resolve it: only
> b-01 and b-02 fail, and the other three are explicitly cleared. **40% is the figure
> the evidence shows, so 40% is the figure quoted.** The restriction is unchanged — it
> was never contingent on which of the two readings held.

## 2. Tier is distance from the text, not difficulty of the words

The re-tier that produced this addendum turned 7 T4 items into 1. The commonest fault,
five of the six, was an item sitting on **one contiguous span and asking for one move
away from it**, dressed up in sophisticated wording.

The sixth, `ENG-002-pp-19`, was tiered T4 on the difficulty of the word *fastidious*
alone. Word difficulty is not a tier signal. It is now T3, with the other
vocabulary-in-context items.

The test that separates T4 from T3:

> **T4 holds two things together.** Either two separated spans whose *relation* is the
> answer, or a pattern recurring across the whole text. T3 makes one move from one place.

Length, rare vocabulary and a long sentence do not make an item T4. They make it hard
to read, which the fairness law is against.

## 3. Both T4 shapes are available; budget roughly two per cluster

**Locatable-span T4** — two separated spans, the relation between them is the answer,
neither span sufficing alone. Census: 18 across the fifteen extracts, 9 across the five
commissioned pieces.

**Whole-text purpose T4** — no line reference, a pattern recurring at three or more
places. Census: 13 of the 15 extracts support one.

**OPERATIONAL DEFINITION, not a judgement call — same category as the comma family's
required-site test and the maths structural-parameter gate: a construction test, not a
feeling.** An item is a whole-text purpose item ONLY IF its key can be stated as a
contrast in two halves:

> "He gives them houses, money and boats, **but** keeps their fur, paws and whiskers."
> "We are told what Tom really feels inside, **while** Ben only sees the outside."
> "He knows things through his feet and body, **but** cannot speak or understand."

A summary can supply one half of a contrast but never both, so a two-halved key is
unreachable from a summary by construction — this is the mechanical guard, and it is
the one that does the work. **If the key cannot be stated as a contrast, the item is
not a purpose item, full stop.** It does not become one because it feels like it
requires understanding, and a one-half key is a FAIL, never a warning to note and
proceed past.

This is the third of five checks in `T4-PURPOSE-BOUNDING-RULE.md`, restated here in
full because it is the check that decides the boundary — the other four (writer-not-
story subject, the plot-summary test, three separate instances, a half-pattern
distractor) narrow a candidate; this one admits or rejects it. Read the full document
before drafting one regardless: the failure mode is sliding into summary, which is a
named non-goal, and the other four checks catch shapes this one alone does not.

### The canonical whole-text item: b-02 (Saltmere)

**Read this one before drafting any whole-text item. It is the CEILING of the type, and
the point of showing it is that the floor is not the target.**

The walk is narrated in the past — but every sentence about something that still exists
slips into the present. **Eight instances, entirely consistent.**

It is canonical rather than merely strong because it asks a **different and harder skill
than any other item in the bank**: noticing a *grammatical* pattern sustained across
eight instances. Every other whole-text item asks the child to notice something about
what the writer SAYS. This one asks her to notice something about how the writing is
BUILT, at a level the prose never comments on.

**And it is unreachable from summary by construction, not by luck** — which is what makes
it the ceiling rather than just the best of a set. Summarising collapses tense choices
into whatever tense the summary happens to use; the pattern cannot survive the act of
summarising. Every other item in the bank passes the plot-summary check because a
summary omits the relevant detail. **This one passes because summarising destroys the
evidence.** That is a stronger guarantee, and it is the standard to aim at.

Note also what it demonstrates about §1: b-02's own `craftNotes.complication` promised a
completely different item, and that promised item FAILS the plot-summary check. The best
item in the bank is one the suggestion field pointed away from.

Combined, the extracts run at **2.07 T4 items per passage**, and every passage yields at
least one. Two extracts that support no span item at all — Wind in the Willows and
Eighty Days, both because the narrator glosses his own points — are rescued by the
whole-text shape, in Wind in the Willows' case by the very double-vision pattern its
glossing narrator never comments on.

Budget two T4 per twelve-item cluster as the working assumption. Three is available on
Black Beauty, Canterville, A Christmas Carol, Three Men in a Boat and Pride and
Prejudice. One is the floor, on Railway Children and Jane Eyre.
