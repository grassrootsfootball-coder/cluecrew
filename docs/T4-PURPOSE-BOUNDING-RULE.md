# The bounding rule for whole-text purpose items

Whole-text purpose is a valid second T4 shape. Summary is a named non-goal (§8).
These are close neighbours, so the boundary has to be checkable rather than felt.
An item is a purpose item only if it passes all five.

## 1. The stem asks about the writer, not the story

PURPOSE asks what the writer is doing to the reader — why it is built this way, what
technique recurs. RECAP asks what happened. The subject of the stem's question is the
test: if the subject is a character or an event, it is drifting.

- Purpose: "Scrooge keeps saying he is not frightened. How does the writer show us that he is?"
- Recap: "What does Scrooge see and hear before the ghost arrives?"

## 2. The plot-summary check

Could someone who had read a plot summary but never the prose reach the key? If yes,
it is recap. This is the single most useful test and it is quick to apply.

## 3. The key is a contrast in two halves

**This is the mechanical guard, and it is the one that does the work.** A summary can
supply one half of a contrast but never both, so a two-halved key is unreachable from
a summary by construction.

- "He gives them houses, money and boats, **but** keeps their fur, paws and whiskers."
- "We are told what Tom really feels inside, **while** Ben only sees the outside."
- "He knows things through his feet and body, **but** cannot speak or understand."

If the key cannot be stated as a contrast, the item is probably recap wearing a
technique stem. Treat a one-half key as a fail, not a warning.

## 4. Three separate instances, none of them sufficient alone

The pattern must show at three or more locations, and no single location may contain
enough to answer. This is what makes it whole-text rather than a span item without a
line reference.

The recurring failure is a **narrator who names his own device at one address**. Where
that happens the item dies even though the pattern is real:

- Jane Eyre, lines 25-26: "No Gytrash was this,—only a traveller." States the mechanism.
- Around the World in Eighty Days, line 73: "preserved a stoical composure."
- Pride and Prejudice, line 39: "What a contrast between him and his friend!"

Check for this before drafting. It killed two of fifteen extracts outright.

## 5. The distractor set has a half-pattern option

A child who noticed one half of the contrast, or read the pattern as content rather
than craft, must have somewhere to go. If no half-pattern distractor exists, the item
is either too easy or not really testing a pattern.

- Wind in the Willows: "He lets them talk to each other like people." (human half only)
- Baskervilles: "Because there is an escaped convict on the moor." (content, not craft)

## Two further constraints from the census

**Watch the preamble.** Black Beauty's preamble states that a horse narrates; Anne's
states that Matthew is shy. Where the preamble gives away the pattern's premise, the
stem must ask about the pattern's *consequence*, or the item collapses to retrieval.

**Check for duplication across passages.** Whole-text patterns repeat across a bank in
a way span items do not. Wind in the Willows and The Jungle Book produce the same
anthropomorphism item almost word for word; Tom Sawyer, Anne and A Christmas Carol are
three variants of "the reader sees what a character cannot"; Canterville and Mill on
the Floss are both register-mismatch comedy. Thirteen viable items across the extracts
carry only nine or ten genuinely distinct techniques. A mock paper drawing several
passages needs to check the technique, not just the item id.
